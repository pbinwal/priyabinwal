 If you fly to Naples, eat good <em>Pizza</em>, remind yourself that in Shakespeare’s Merchant of Venice, Portia speaks of the Neapolitan prince as a ‘colt indeed’, chuckle, and continue further south along the coast, you will find yourself upon the Amalfi coast of Italy. If you are willing, you can take a bus to the town of Bomerano and hike (mostly downhill) for a few hours along the <em>Sentiero degli Dei</em> or the Path of the Gods. After catching some stunning views of the Tyrrhenian Sea during the hike, you can hike down to finally reach the beautiful town of Positano.
  <br>
  <br>
Also nicknamed <em>città verticale</em> or the vertical city, the cliffside town of Positano features multiple vertically stacked colourful houses and dramatic views of the Tyrrhenian Sea. In summer, the water is just right to wade into, almost like a refreshing reward for your calves after having brought you down over 1500 stone steps that one must descend at the end of the hike to get to this beautiful town.

 If you aren’t broke or on a PhD student salary, you may be able to afford a ridiculously overpriced sunbed. Else just remember to carry what Douglas Adams described as <em>“about the most massively useful thing an interstellar (international in this case) hitchhiker can have"</em>— a towel. The water is clear and sparkles on sunny days, and if you, like me, happen to have watched the Odyssey recently, you might find yourself looking at the seemingly boundless expanse of the sea and wondering how brave those humans must have been who strapped themselves to wooden rafts and dared to set sail to find out what lay on the other side.

  <br>
  <br>
Speaking of Douglas Adams, I often think about how he describes bypasses in the opening pages of The Hitchhiker's Guide to the Galaxy. <em>“Bypasses are devices that allow some people to dash from point A to point B very fast while other people dash from point B to point A very fast. People living at point C, being a point directly in between, are often given to wonder what's so great about point A that so many people from point B are so keen to get there, and what's so great about point B that so many people from point A are so keen to get there. They often wish that people would just once and for all work out where the hell they wanted to be.”</em>
  <br>
  <br>
I think the same could be said about human travel to other countries. Think of the Europeans sunbathing on the sandy beaches of Phuket or visiting the royal places of Jaipur while the Asians from those countries are busy clicking pictures with the Eiffel Tower or admiring the beautiful architecture of Rome. All in all, we are always seeking a dash of novelty, and we have convinced ourselves that we are unlikely to find it close to home. Ask a human living in the most picturesque and stunning
 landscape possible to name their dream destination, and chances are they'll never talk about something close to home. 
  <br>
  <br>
<span class="highlight-prose">We humans possess the remarkable ability to get used to just about anything. We walk the world not only numb to the horrors that pervade it but also quite agnostic to all things of beauty and light. A thing of beauty is not a joy forever, after all.</span>
  <br>
  <br>
And there's nothing wrong with travel in itself, of course. I do condone visiting new places with all my heart. But I also think that in the exercise of getting habituated to things of beauty around us, we more often than not forget to pause and appreciate the beauty that surrounds us all the time.
  <br>
  <br>
Go outside, if you are not outside already, and look up. Do you see a cloud? Yes, that amorphous mass of water and dust which rises from the earth up to the sky, and moves with the wind. Let your gaze trace its ever-changing outline and let your mind conjure a form that it thinks has materialised. Or maybe don't think of a form at all, but just watch it glide away gently in the wind. Cloud after cloud, coming and going. 
  <br>
  <br>
Lay down if you will, on the green grass that is still a little wet from the morning dew. Then pick up a blade and hold it against the sky. Admire the sharp contrast between the green and the azure sky and the white fluff of the clouds. 
  <br>
  <br>
Remind yourself of the contrast between the vastness of the sky against the blade of grass you hold in your hands, and then the vastness of the whole of existence against the impunity of our human life.

This may sound like something you'd hear in a hypnotic meditation session, but bear with me.. Because perhaps it is the case that in wondering whether the grass really is greener on the other side, maybe we've ignored what a fortune it is to have any grass at all on our side for too long.
  <br>
  <br>
 <span class="unwarranted-title">Unwarranted opinion:</span> I will never understand why humans will pay huge sums to get to the top of 100 ft towers for a view but then doze off while sitting on window seats in airplanes cruising at altitudes of several thousand feet in the sky.
  <br>
  <br>
 <span class="unwarranted-title">Unwarranted paragraph:</span> Why do I write? On most days it's not because I like it, even though I do really love it, but because I can't really help not doing it. You see, writing for me comes from a place of being overwhelmed. It happens when a thought that is so powerful that it demands being inked (or encoded in Unicode) comes to me, and I have no choice but to comply. That is also to say that the less of me there is in the process, the better the writing is. It is when I'm not the writer but the medium that I produce something worthwhile. So in a way, writing (or good writing, if I may be the judge) is not really a choice or even something I do of my own accord but something that happens to me. You know what John Green said about pain, how it demands to be felt? I feel that some thoughts or emotions demand to be written down in the same way.
